# Morning Physics Briefing 05
## 
Dielectric Response, LO–TO Splitting, and the Origin of Reststrahlen Bands

Monday, 19 October 2026 | Week 1 — Essential Solid-State Foundations | Reading time: 9–10 minutes

**Today's central question:** Why can two optical phonons involving essentially the same atomic displacement pattern have different frequencies, and how does this difference create the electromagnetic conditions necessary for phonon polaritons?

Today completes the first five foundational briefings. We move from the mechanical description of lattice vibrations to their electrodynamic consequences.

This is a particularly important transition for multilayer van der Waals heterostructures. The optical phonons of polar crystals do not merely respond to electromagnetic fields; their collective ionic displacements generate electric fields that modify the vibrations themselves.

This feedback produces longitudinal–transverse optical phonon splitting, strongly dispersive dielectric functions, and Reststrahlen bands.

We will also examine a subtle but essential result: **LO–TO splitting behaves fundamentally differently in an isolated two-dimensional crystal than in a three-dimensional bulk crystal.**

That distinction becomes especially important when interpreting graphene/hBN heterostructures, atomically thin polar layers, and phonon-polariton experiments.

## 1. From mechanical vibrations to electrically active phonons

In our earlier briefings, we described phonons as collective atomic vibrations determined by the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the dynamical matrix.

That description is sufficient for understanding many aspects of lattice dynamics.

However, polar crystals require an additional ingredient.

When atoms carry nonzero effective charges, their displacements can produce changes in macroscopic electric polarization.

Consider a simple polar crystal with two oppositely charged sublattices.

If the sublattices move in opposite directions, their relative displacement produces an oscillating electric dipole.

The resulting polarization generates an electric field.

That electric field acts back on the charged atoms, modifying the restoring forces associated with the vibration.

We therefore have two contributions to the phonon dynamics.

The first is the short-range mechanical interaction between atoms.

The second is the long-range electrostatic interaction generated by the collective polarization.

The latter is responsible for several characteristic features of polar optical phonons.

**The important physical insight is that the restoring force of a polar optical phonon depends not only on atomic bonding but also on the electric field generated by the vibration itself.**

This field depends on the direction of the phonon displacement relative to its wavevector.

That observation leads directly to LO–TO splitting.

## 2. What distinguishes longitudinal and transverse optical phonons?

Consider a polar optical phonon with a small but finite wavevector.

For a longitudinal optical (LO) phonon, the polarization induced by the atomic displacement has a component parallel to the phonon wavevector.

For a transverse optical (TO) phonon, the induced polarization is perpendicular to the propagation direction in the simplest isotropic description.

This distinction is particularly clear in a cubic polar crystal.

Imagine neighboring positively and negatively charged sublattices moving relative to one another.

For a longitudinal displacement pattern, the polarization varies along the direction of the polarization itself.

This produces a nonzero polarization charge density.

The resulting macroscopic electric field creates an additional restoring force.

For a purely transverse displacement, the polarization is perpendicular to the wavevector.

In an ideal isotropic bulk crystal, this displacement does not generate the same longitudinal macroscopic electric field.

Consequently, the two vibrations experience different restoring forces.

The longitudinal mode generally has a higher frequency than the corresponding transverse mode.

This is the physical origin of LO–TO splitting.

There is an important qualification for anisotropic materials.

In a general crystal, the atomic displacement vector, induced polarization, and phonon wavevector need not be parallel or perpendicular in the simple isotropic sense.

The relevant distinction is whether the mode generates a longitudinal macroscopic electric field.

The resulting frequency splitting can therefore depend on propagation direction.

**LO–TO splitting is an electrodynamic correction to lattice dynamics, not simply a consequence of stronger or weaker chemical bonds.**

## 3. Born effective charges: The microscopic connection

The electric polarization generated by an atomic displacement depends on the atom's Born effective charge.

The Born effective charge is not simply the nominal ionic charge assigned through chemical valence.

It describes how the macroscopic polarization changes when an atom is displaced while the macroscopic electric field is held fixed.

In a general crystal, the Born effective charge is a tensor.

This is important because the induced polarization need not point in the same direction as the atomic displacement.

In a layered material, the response to in-plane displacement can differ substantially from the response to out-of-plane displacement.

The Born effective charges therefore help determine which phonons couple strongly to electromagnetic fields and how that coupling depends on direction.

They also enter the long-range, nonanalytic contribution to the dynamical matrix near the Brillouin-zone center.

For a polar bulk crystal, that contribution remains finite as the phonon wavevector approaches zero along a specified direction.

This is why longitudinal and transverse optical phonon frequencies can remain different in the long-wavelength limit of a three-dimensional polar crystal.

Later, we will see why the corresponding result changes in two dimensions.

## 4. The dielectric function as a driven lattice response

We can now connect the phonon dynamics to optical measurements.

Imagine applying an oscillating electric field to a polar crystal.

The field exerts forces on the charged atomic sublattices.

At low frequencies, the ions can follow the applied field approximately in phase.

As the driving frequency approaches an infrared-active optical phonon resonance, the ionic displacement becomes strongly enhanced.

Above the resonance, the ionic motion changes phase relative to the driving field.

The resulting polarization is therefore frequency-dependent.

This produces a dispersive dielectric response.

For a principal crystallographic direction, a simple single-oscillator model is

<div class="equation">
\[
\boxed{ \varepsilon_j(\omega) = \varepsilon_{\infty,j} + \frac{S_j} {\omega_{\mathrm{TO},j}^{2}-\omega^{2}-i\gamma_j\omega} }
\]
</div>

Here:

- **ε\_j(ω)** is the complex dielectric function along principal axis j.

- **ε∞,j** is the high-frequency dielectric constant associated with electronic polarization.

- **S_j** is the oscillator strength of the infrared-active phonon.

- **ω\_TO,j** is the transverse-optical phonon angular frequency.

- **ω** is the applied electromagnetic angular frequency.

- **γ\_j** is the phenomenological damping rate.

The model assumes one dominant infrared-active oscillator for the chosen polarization.

Real crystals can possess several infrared-active phonons, requiring multiple oscillator contributions.

The oscillator strength is related to the mode-effective charge, atomic masses, and vibrational eigenvector.

A mode with a large effective charge can contribute strongly to the dielectric response.

A mode with little induced polarization may have a small infrared oscillator strength even if its atomic displacement is substantial.

**What happens near the TO frequency?**

Near the transverse-optical phonon resonance, the denominator becomes small.

The ionic polarization responds strongly to the driving electric field.

The imaginary part of the dielectric function becomes large, indicating significant optical absorption.

The real part changes rapidly across the resonance.

Under sufficiently weak damping, it can become negative immediately above the transverse-optical phonon frequency.

This is the essential mechanism behind the Reststrahlen response of polar crystals.

## 5. The Lyddane–Sachs–Teller relation

The relationship between the longitudinal and transverse optical phonon frequencies can be expressed through the Lyddane–Sachs–Teller relation.

For an insulating three-dimensional crystal with one relevant polar oscillator along a principal direction, in the lossless limit,

<div class="equation">
\[
\boxed{ \frac{\varepsilon_{0,j}}{\varepsilon_{\infty,j}} = \frac{\omega_{\mathrm{LO},j}^{2}} {\omega_{\mathrm{TO},j}^{2}} }
\]
</div>

The precise band boundaries depend on the dielectric model, crystal properties, and experimental conditions.

The important point is that the in-plane and out-of-plane dielectric components have opposite signs within these intervals.

This makes hBN a natural hyperbolic material.

The review by Caldwell and colleagues provides a detailed account of these optical properties and their implications for nanophotonics. <u>[Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41578-019-0124-1?utm_source=chatgpt.com)</u>

**Why is this remarkable?**

In an ordinary isotropic dielectric, electromagnetic propagation is characterized by a single scalar permittivity.

In hBN, the electric field can experience qualitatively different responses depending on its direction relative to the layers.

Within a hyperbolic band, the material can support electromagnetic excitations with very large wavevectors.

These are the foundations of hyperbolic phonon polaritons.

However, one should distinguish the dielectric response of bulk hBN from the actual modes supported by a finite-thickness flake.

A thin hBN slab has electromagnetic boundary conditions at its upper and lower surfaces.

These select discrete guided polaritonic branches whose wavelengths depend on thickness and surrounding materials.

Consequently, the phonon-polariton dispersion can change substantially even when the underlying optical phonon frequencies remain nearly unchanged.

This distinction will become central in Week 3.

## 8. The surprising result: LO–TO splitting changes in two dimensions

We now arrive at one of today's most important research-level points.

The familiar finite LO–TO splitting at the Brillouin-zone center is a result associated with the long-range Coulomb interaction in three-dimensional polar crystals.

What happens when the crystal becomes atomically thin?

Consider an isolated polar monolayer.

A longitudinal optical vibration produces an oscillating in-plane polarization.

That polarization generates an electric field, just as in a bulk crystal.

However, the spatial behavior of the electric field is different.

In a three-dimensional crystal, the polarization charge is distributed throughout a bulk medium.

In an isolated monolayer, the induced charge is confined to a two-dimensional sheet, while the electric field extends into the surrounding three-dimensional space.

This changes the long-wavelength electrostatic interaction.

For an ideal isolated polar monolayer, the nonanalytic LO–TO frequency difference vanishes as the in-plane phonon wavevector approaches zero.

The longitudinal and transverse optical modes therefore become degenerate at the zone center.

But the longitudinal branch generally develops a different slope from the transverse branch near that point.

**The disappearance of a finite zone-center LO–TO splitting does not mean that the long-range polar interaction has disappeared.**

Instead, the interaction manifests itself through the wavevector dependence of the longitudinal phonon dispersion.

This is a fundamentally different long-wavelength behavior from that of a three-dimensional bulk polar crystal.

**Why does the surrounding dielectric matter?**

Because the electric field generated by the monolayer extends outside the atomic sheet, its electrostatic energy depends on the surrounding environment.

Placing the monolayer on a dielectric substrate modifies the Coulomb interaction.

Encapsulating it between other materials modifies the interaction again.

Introducing a nearby conducting layer can provide additional screening.

Consequently, the longitudinal optical phonon dispersion of a polar monolayer can depend on the dielectric properties of neighboring layers.

This is especially important for van der Waals heterostructures.

A change in the surrounding material can alter the long-wavelength LO dispersion without requiring a large change in the local chemical bonding.

This result was analyzed quantitatively by Sohier and colleagues using two-dimensional electrostatic models and first-principles calculations.

Their work established the characteristic zone-center degeneracy and longitudinal-branch slope, including the dependence on layer number and dielectric environment. <u>[American Chemical Society Publications](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/abs/10.1021/acs.nanolett.7b01090?utm_source=chatgpt.com)</u>

## 9. Application: A graphene/hBN heterostructure

Consider a heterostructure consisting of a polar hBN layer adjacent to graphene.

This provides a particularly useful example because the two materials play different roles.

The hBN layer contains polar optical phonons.

Graphene contains mobile electronic carriers whose density can be controlled through electrostatic gating.

These carriers can screen electric fields generated by the polar lattice vibrations.

**What changes when graphene is introduced?**

The short-range mechanical interactions within hBN need not change dramatically.

However, the long-range electric field associated with a longitudinal optical phonon can be modified by the neighboring graphene.

The resulting phonon dispersion can therefore change through electrostatic screening.

This is not the same mechanism as changing the interlayer force constants associated with shear or breathing modes.

For the latter, the relevant restoring forces are primarily associated with relative mechanical displacement of the layers.

For the polar LO mode, the long-range Coulomb interaction can play a major role.

These mechanisms can coexist but should be analyzed separately.

**What if graphene is electrically gated?**

Changing the graphene carrier density changes its electronic screening response.

At finite frequency and wavevector, the screening is generally dynamical rather than purely static.

The graphene plasmon response can also interact with the electromagnetic fields associated with hBN phonons.

This can produce hybrid plasmon–phonon-polariton modes.

It is therefore important to distinguish two questions.

**Has the underlying hBN phonon dispersion changed because of electrostatic screening?**

**Or has the measured electromagnetic mode changed because graphene plasmons and hBN phonon polaritons have hybridized?**

Both mechanisms can influence an experiment, but they are not identical.

A proper interpretation requires a model that distinguishes the lattice response from the full electromagnetic response of the heterostructure.

This is one reason graphene/hBN is such a useful system for studying the connection between microscopic phonon physics and macroscopic electrodynamics.

## 10. How would these effects appear experimentally?

Suppose you fabricate an hBN flake and measure its optical response using three complementary techniques.

**Far-field infrared spectroscopy**

An infrared spectrum can reveal strong resonances associated with infrared-active optical phonons.

The TO frequencies are closely connected to resonant absorption.

The LO frequencies influence the zeros of the dielectric function and can be accessed through suitable longitudinally sensitive measurements or optical modeling.

The Reststrahlen bands appear as characteristic spectral intervals with strong dielectric dispersion and often enhanced reflectivity.

However, the exact spectral features depend on sample geometry, polarization, and surrounding media.

A reflectivity maximum should not automatically be identified with a phonon eigenfrequency.

**Raman spectroscopy**

Raman spectroscopy probes how phonon displacements modify the electronic polarizability.

It can identify phonon symmetries and provide information about strain, stacking, and structural changes.

However, the Raman-active modes are not necessarily the same modes that dominate the infrared dielectric response.

In a centrosymmetric crystal, the usual Raman and electric-dipole infrared selection rules can even be mutually exclusive.

This means that a Raman spectrum alone is generally insufficient to determine the dielectric oscillator strengths relevant for phonon polaritons.

**Scattering-type scanning near-field optical microscopy**

In s-SNOM, a sharp metallic tip provides highly localized electromagnetic fields containing large in-plane momenta.

These fields can couple to phonon-polariton modes that are difficult to excite directly with far-field illumination.

By imaging the near-field response at different infrared frequencies, one can extract information about polariton propagation, confinement, and damping.

A key experimental distinction is that near-field fringes measure properties of the coupled electromagnetic mode.

They do not directly provide the bare LO or TO phonon dispersion.

Interpreting the images therefore requires an electrodynamic model of the complete sample, including thickness, substrate, and dielectric tensor.

## 11. Today's principal interpretive pitfall

Misconception: The LO–TO frequency difference is an intrinsic constant of a polar material, independent of dimensionality and environment.

This is incorrect.

In a three-dimensional bulk polar crystal, long-range electrostatic interactions can produce a finite LO–TO splitting in the long-wavelength limit.

The magnitude depends on mode-effective charges, dielectric screening, and the relevant propagation direction.

In an isolated two-dimensional polar monolayer, the corresponding long-wavelength behavior is qualitatively different.

The LO and TO modes become degenerate at the zone center, while their dispersions can have different slopes.

The surrounding dielectric environment influences this slope.

In multilayers, the electrostatic response evolves with the number of layers, and the individual vibrational branches can also hybridize through interlayer coupling.

Thus, when comparing bulk hBN, few-layer hBN, and a monolayer embedded in a heterostructure, one should not automatically apply the same bulk LO–TO splitting picture to all three.

There is a related computational pitfall.

A three-dimensional periodic supercell calculation of an isolated monolayer can introduce artificial electrostatic interactions between periodically repeated images.

Without an appropriate treatment of the two-dimensional Coulomb interaction, the calculated long-wavelength optical phonon dispersion can be misleading.

This is especially important when comparing density-functional perturbation theory calculations with experimental measurements on atomically thin polar materials.

## 12. Research questions

Question 1 — Why is the LO phonon frequency generally higher than the TO frequency in a simple bulk polar crystal?

Answer: A longitudinal polar optical vibration generates a macroscopic electric field associated with its polarization charge.

This field contributes an additional restoring force to the lattice motion.

A transverse optical vibration does not generate the same longitudinal field in the isotropic bulk limit.

The longitudinal mode therefore generally has a higher frequency.

The difference is controlled by the long-range Coulomb interaction and the dynamical charges of the vibrating atoms.

**Question 2 — Why does a Reststrahlen band appear between the TO and LO frequencies?**

Answer: Near the TO resonance, the ionic polarization responds strongly to the applied electromagnetic field.

Above that resonance, the ionic contribution to the dielectric function can become sufficiently negative to overcome the positive electronic background.

In the ideal single-oscillator model, the real dielectric function is negative between the TO and LO frequencies.

This produces strong reflection in an appropriate bulk optical geometry and enables confined phonon-polaritonic modes under suitable electromagnetic boundary conditions.

**Question 3 — A monolayer hBN sample shows no finite LO–TO frequency splitting at the zone center. Does this mean its optical phonons are nonpolar?**

Answer: No.

The absence of a finite zone-center splitting is consistent with the long-wavelength electrostatics of an isolated two-dimensional polar crystal.

The phonons can still possess substantial mode-effective charges and generate electric fields.

Their polar character appears through the finite-wavevector LO dispersion and its sensitivity to dielectric screening.

The key distinction is between the existence of polar coupling and the specific wavevector dependence of the resulting electrostatic restoring force.

**Question 4 — A graphene/hBN heterostructure exhibits a gate-dependent infrared near-field resonance. Can you conclude that graphene has changed the hBN optical phonon frequency?**

Answer: Not from that observation alone.

The gate-dependent resonance may reflect a change in graphene conductivity, plasmon dispersion, or plasmon–phonon-polariton hybridization.

The bare phonon frequencies could remain nearly unchanged.

Establishing a genuine phonon-frequency shift requires separating the lattice contribution from the electromagnetic mode structure.

A useful analysis would combine independent phonon spectroscopy, near-field dispersion measurements, and calculations incorporating the frequency- and momentum-dependent electronic response of graphene.

## 14. What you should retain from today's briefing

Today's central advance is the connection between lattice vibrations and macroscopic electromagnetic response.

A polar optical phonon produces an oscillating electric polarization.

The electric field generated by that polarization modifies the lattice restoring forces.

In a three-dimensional polar crystal, this can produce a finite long-wavelength LO–TO splitting.

The same lattice polarization produces a strongly frequency-dependent dielectric function.

Near the transverse-optical phonon resonance, the dielectric response can become negative over a frequency interval extending approximately toward the longitudinal-optical phonon frequency.

This creates a Reststrahlen band.

In anisotropic layered crystals such as hBN, different dielectric tensor components can become negative in different frequency intervals.

That anisotropy provides the foundation for hyperbolic phonon polaritons.

However, the long-wavelength electrostatics changes in two dimensions.

An isolated polar monolayer does not generally exhibit the same finite zone-center LO–TO splitting as its three-dimensional bulk counterpart.

Instead, the longitudinal optical phonon dispersion becomes sensitive to wavevector and dielectric environment.

This is particularly important for heterostructures, where neighboring layers can alter the electrostatic response through screening and electromagnetic hybridization.

**The most important principle is that polar phonon physics depends on three interconnected ingredients: lattice restoring forces, dynamical charges, and electrostatic boundary conditions.**

Understanding their separate contributions is essential for interpreting phonons and phonon polaritons in twisted multilayer materials.
