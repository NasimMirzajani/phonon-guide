# Morning Physics Briefing 08
## Moiré Reciprocal Lattices: How Twist Creates New Phonon Wavevectors

**Thursday, 22 October 2026 | Week 2 — Multilayer and Twist Physics | Reading time: 9–10 minutes**

**Today's central question:** How does rotating one atomic layer relative to another create a new reciprocal-space structure, and why can this make phonons with finite crystal momentum observable in Raman spectroscopy?

Yesterday, we examined how stacking registry determines interlayer force constants, crystal symmetry, and atomic reconstruction.

Today, we move from real space to reciprocal space.

A twisted bilayer contains two atomic lattices with different orientations. Their superposition produces a spatial modulation of local stacking registry, characterized by a length scale that can be much larger than the atomic lattice constant.

This is the origin of the moiré superlattice.

The corresponding reciprocal-space vectors are much smaller than the atomic reciprocal lattice vectors. They introduce an additional momentum scale into the vibrational problem.

That momentum scale is particularly important for phonons.

It determines how phonon branches are folded into a reduced Brillouin zone, which finite-wavevector vibrations can become Raman-active, and which phonon modes may hybridize through spatially modulated interlayer coupling.

Our goal today is to construct the moiré reciprocal lattice and understand its experimental consequences.

---

## 1. Begin with two reciprocal lattices

Consider an isolated monolayer of MoS₂.

Its atomic structure is periodic under translations by two primitive lattice vectors.

The corresponding reciprocal lattice is generated by two primitive reciprocal vectors.

These vectors determine the Brillouin zone and provide the natural coordinates for describing phonon wavevectors.

A phonon with wavevector **q** has a displacement pattern whose phase changes from one unit cell to another.

Its wavevector determines the spatial variation of the collective vibration.

Now place a second MoS₂ monolayer on top of the first.

If the two layers have identical crystallographic orientations and lattice constants, their reciprocal lattices coincide.

A lateral translation changes their stacking registry but leaves the orientations of their reciprocal lattices unchanged.

Now rotate the upper layer by a small angle.

Its reciprocal lattice rotates by the same angle.

The two sets of reciprocal lattice vectors no longer coincide.

Their differences define a new, much smaller set of reciprocal-space vectors.

These are the **moiré reciprocal lattice vectors**.

The essential physical idea is that a small angular difference between two large reciprocal lattice vectors can produce a much smaller difference vector.

That small difference vector corresponds to a long spatial wavelength.

---

## 2. Constructing the moiré reciprocal lattice

Consider two identical hexagonal lattices with lattice constant \\(a\\).

Let their relative twist angle be \\(\theta\\).

The primitive reciprocal lattice vectors of the second layer are rotated relative to those of the first.

We define the moiré reciprocal vectors by taking their differences.

For identical lattices undergoing a rigid relative rotation,

<div class="equation">

\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{g}_i
&=
\mathbf{b}_i^{(2)}
-
\mathbf{b}_i^{(1)}
\\[6pt]
|\mathbf{g}_i|
&=
2|\mathbf{b}_i|
\sin\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)
\\[6pt]
L_{\mathrm{m}}
&=
\frac{a}{2\sin(\theta/2)}
\end{aligned}
}
\]

</div>

Here:

- \\(\mathbf{b}_i^{(1)}\\) and \\(\mathbf{b}_i^{(2)}\\) are corresponding primitive reciprocal lattice vectors of the two layers.
- \\(\mathbf{g}_i\\) is a primitive moiré reciprocal vector.
- \\(\theta\\) is the relative twist angle.
- \\(a\\) is the atomic lattice constant.
- \\(L_{\mathrm{m}}\\) is the moiré lattice constant, or the distance between equivalent points of the geometrical moiré pattern.

For a hexagonal lattice, the magnitude of a primitive reciprocal lattice vector is \\(4\pi/(\sqrt{3}a)\\).

At small twist angles, the moiré period increases approximately inversely with the angle expressed in radians.

### Physical interpretation

Imagine two nearly parallel sets of equally spaced lines.

When the lines are perfectly aligned, their periodic patterns coincide.

If one set is rotated slightly, the relative alignment changes slowly across space.

The resulting large-scale pattern is a spatial beat between the two underlying periodicities.

The same principle applies to two-dimensional atomic lattices.

The moiré reciprocal vectors describe the spatial frequencies of that beat pattern.

**A smaller twist angle produces a larger moiré period and correspondingly smaller moiré reciprocal vectors.**

This relationship is the foundation of moiré momentum engineering.

---

## 3. A numerical example: Twisted bilayer MoS₂

Consider two MoS₂ monolayers with an atomic lattice constant of approximately 0.316 nm.

Rotate one layer by 1° relative to the other.

The geometrical moiré period is approximately **18.1 nm**.

This is about 57 times larger than the atomic lattice constant.

The magnitude of a primitive moiré reciprocal vector is approximately **0.40 nm⁻¹**.

Compare this with the distance from the atomic Brillouin-zone center to its K point, approximately **13.3 nm⁻¹**.

The moiré reciprocal vector is much smaller than the characteristic atomic Brillouin-zone dimensions.

Now decrease the twist angle to 0.5°.

The moiré period increases to approximately 36.2 nm.

Increase the twist angle to 2°.

The moiré period decreases to approximately 9.1 nm.

| Twist angle | Moiré period | Primitive moiré reciprocal-vector magnitude |
|---|---:|---:|
| 0.5° | 36.2 nm | 0.20 nm⁻¹ |
| 1.0° | 18.1 nm | 0.40 nm⁻¹ |
| 2.0° | 9.1 nm | 0.80 nm⁻¹ |

These values assume identical, unstrained hexagonal lattices and rigid rotation.

The important consequence is that a relatively small change in twist angle can substantially change the momentum scale introduced by the moiré structure.

This gives us a way to select different portions of the phonon dispersion through twist.

## 4. The moiré Brillouin zone

Recall that the [Brillouin zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brillouin_zone) is the primitive cell of the reciprocal lattice.

For an isolated MoS₂ monolayer, the Brillouin zone is determined by the atomic lattice periodicity.

Its dimensions are therefore set by the atomic lattice constant.

For a commensurate twisted bilayer, the combined structure has a larger real-space unit cell.

The corresponding reciprocal-space Brillouin zone is smaller.

We call this the **moiré Brillouin zone**, or mini-Brillouin zone.

### Why does the Brillouin zone become smaller?

A larger real-space unit cell means that fewer distinct wavevectors are needed to describe the periodic structure.

Wavevectors that differ by a reciprocal vector of the enlarged supercell are equivalent under its translational symmetry.

Consequently, phonon branches that occupied different positions in the original atomic Brillouin zone can be represented within the same reduced zone.

This is the geometrical origin of zone folding.

### An important qualification: Commensurability

A generic twisted bilayer is not necessarily exactly periodic.

For certain twist angles, the two atomic lattices form an exact common supercell.

These are commensurate structures, for which an exact moiré Brillouin zone can be defined.

At a generic incommensurate angle, there is no finite common atomic supercell.

Nevertheless, the local stacking registry can still exhibit a well-defined long-wavelength moiré modulation.

The associated difference vectors remain useful for describing its dominant spatial frequencies.

In theoretical calculations, one often uses commensurate approximants or continuum models to treat such structures.

**When reading a paper, distinguish an exact supercell Brillouin zone from the effective moiré reciprocal-space description of an incommensurate interface.**

This becomes particularly important when evaluating calculated phonon band structures.

---

## 5. What happens to phonon dispersion when the Brillouin zone shrinks?

Consider the phonon dispersion of an isolated MoS₂ monolayer.

Each phonon branch has a frequency that depends on its wavevector.

In the atomic Brillouin zone, the acoustic branches begin at zero frequency at Γ and increase as the wavevector moves away from the zone center.

Optical branches generally have finite frequencies at Γ and may disperse upward or downward toward the zone boundaries.

Now construct a large moiré supercell.

The reduced Brillouin zone contains many wavevectors that were distinct in the original atomic description.

When we represent the original phonon dispersion in this smaller zone, portions of the branches are folded back toward the new zone center.

The result is a larger number of phonon branches within a smaller reciprocal-space region.

### A useful mental picture

Imagine drawing a phonon dispersion across the original atomic Brillouin zone.

Now divide the horizontal wavevector axis into smaller intervals corresponding to the moiré reciprocal periodicity.

Translate those intervals into the reduced zone.

Several pieces of the original dispersion now appear at the same reduced wavevector.

This is zone folding.

In an exact commensurate supercell, these folded branches are legitimate representations of the lattice eigenmodes.

However, the folding operation itself does not necessarily change their frequencies.

It changes how the modes are labeled in reciprocal space.

Physical changes to the frequencies require changes in the dynamical matrix, such as interlayer coupling, strain, or reconstruction.

This distinction is central to interpreting moiré phonons.

---

## 6. Why zone folding matters for Raman spectroscopy

In conventional first-order [Raman scattering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raman_scattering), the momentum transferred by visible photons is small compared with the dimensions of an atomic Brillouin zone.

For example, the wavevector magnitude of a 532 nm photon in vacuum is approximately 0.012 nm⁻¹.

The maximum momentum transfer in a backscattering geometry is of order twice this value.

By comparison, the primitive moiré reciprocal vector in our 1° twisted MoS₂ example is approximately 0.40 nm⁻¹.

The atomic Brillouin-zone dimensions are larger still.

In an ordinary monolayer, first-order Raman scattering therefore predominantly probes phonons close to the atomic Γ point.

But the moiré superlattice changes the relevant translational symmetry.

A phonon that originally had finite wavevector in the monolayer description may fold to the moiré-zone center.

Under suitable conditions, it can then contribute to first-order Raman scattering.

The approximate momentum-selection condition is

<div class="equation">

\[
\boxed{
\mathbf{q}_{\mathrm{ph}}
+
n_1\mathbf{g}_1
+
n_2\mathbf{g}_2
\simeq
\mathbf{q}_{\mathrm{opt}}
}
\]

</div>

Here:

- \\(\mathbf{q}_{\mathrm{ph}}\\) is the phonon wavevector in the original layer-based description.
- \\(\mathbf{g}_1\\) and \\(\mathbf{g}_2\\) are primitive moiré reciprocal vectors.
- \\(n_1\\) and \\(n_2\\) are integers.
- \\(\mathbf{q}_{\mathrm{opt}}\\) is the momentum transferred by the incident and scattered photons.

The relation is understood modulo the appropriate atomic reciprocal lattice vectors.

For conventional visible Raman spectroscopy, the optical momentum transfer is usually small compared with the moiré reciprocal vectors.

Thus, phonons with wavevectors approximately equal to combinations of moiré reciprocal vectors can be folded into the optically accessible region.

### Why this is useful

The twist angle controls the moiré reciprocal vectors.

Those vectors determine which finite-wavevector phonons can fold toward the moiré-zone center.

By changing the twist angle, we can therefore change the phonon wavevectors sampled through moiré-assisted Raman scattering.

This provides a route to obtaining information about finite-wavevector phonon dispersion using an optical experiment.

However, the corresponding Raman matrix elements must be sufficiently large.

A folded phonon is not guaranteed to produce an observable peak.

## 7. The essential distinction: Folding versus coupling

We should now separate two effects that are often discussed together.

### Effect 1: Geometrical zone folding

Suppose we construct a supercell without changing the underlying interatomic force constants.

The same physical phonon modes can be represented using a smaller Brillouin zone.

Their wavevectors are relabeled.

The total number of phonon branches within the reduced zone increases because the enlarged unit cell contains more atoms.

This is the geometrical consequence of changing the periodic description.

### Effect 2: Dynamical coupling

Now introduce a spatially modulated interlayer interaction.

The local restoring forces vary across the moiré structure.

This modulation enters the dynamical matrix.

It can couple phonon displacement patterns whose wavevectors differ by moiré reciprocal vectors.

When two such modes have similar frequencies and compatible symmetry, they may hybridize.

The resulting eigenvectors become mixtures of the original vibrational patterns.

Their frequencies can shift, and avoided crossings can appear in the phonon dispersion.

The mixing can also redistribute Raman intensity among the resulting modes.

**Zone folding determines which phonon states can be represented at the same reduced wavevector. Dynamical coupling determines how those states interact.**

This distinction is particularly important when interpreting a new Raman peak.

Its appearance may involve both folding and changes in optical matrix elements.

A frequency shift may additionally reflect genuine modifications to the lattice dynamics.

## 8. Experimental demonstration: Moiré phonons in twisted bilayer MoS₂

A foundational experiment was reported by Lin and colleagues in 2018.

They investigated twisted bilayer MoS₂ using Raman spectroscopy.

The authors identified additional Raman features associated with the moiré superlattice.

These features were interpreted as phonons folded from finite wavevectors of the constituent monolayer into the moiré-zone center.

An important result was that the frequencies of the moiré-related Raman peaks varied with twist angle.

Because the twist angle determines the moiré reciprocal vectors, this provided a way to associate the observed phonon frequencies with different points of the monolayer phonon dispersion.

The authors also showed that the Raman intensity of these features was strongly enhanced when the excitation energy approached the C-exciton resonance of MoS₂.

This is an important experimental qualification.

The visibility of moiré phonons depends on the electronic resonance conditions as well as the vibrational structure.

A weak or absent peak at one laser wavelength may become prominent at another.

The work combined Raman measurements with first-principles calculations to connect the additional spectral features to spatially modulated interlayer coupling.

### What does this experiment establish?

It demonstrates that a moiré superlattice can modify the Raman-accessible vibrational spectrum.

It also provides evidence that twist-dependent Raman peaks can carry information about finite-wavevector phonons.

However, interpreting a particular peak requires identifying the corresponding phonon branch and accounting for the optical matrix element.

The measured intensity is influenced by resonance enhancement, symmetry, and mode mixing.

Thus, the frequency dependence on twist angle is particularly informative when combined with calculated phonon dispersions.

## 9. A heterobilayer example: MoS₂/WSe₂

So far, we have considered two identical lattices rotated relative to one another.

Now consider a MoS₂/WSe₂ heterobilayer.

The two materials have different atomic lattice constants.

Representative values are approximately 0.316 nm for MoS₂ and 0.328 nm for WSe₂.

The lattice mismatch is therefore approximately 3.8%.

This produces a moiré modulation even when the crystallographic orientations are aligned.

For an ideal, unstrained, aligned heterobilayer, the resulting moiré period is approximately **8.6 nm**.

This is consistent with experimental scanning tunneling microscopy measurements of aligned MoS₂/WSe₂ heterobilayers, which found a moiré periodicity of approximately 8.7 nm.

### What happens when we introduce a small twist?

The moiré reciprocal vectors now depend on both lattice mismatch and relative rotation.

For small mismatch and twist angle, the inverse moiré length is approximately proportional to the square root of the sum of the squared fractional lattice mismatch and squared twist angle in radians.

For the representative MoS₂/WSe₂ lattice constants above, introducing a 1° twist reduces the ideal geometrical moiré period from approximately 8.6 nm to about 7.5 nm.

This differs substantially from the MoS₂ homobilayer.

In the homobilayer, the moiré period diverges as the twist angle approaches zero.

In the mismatched heterobilayer, the moiré period remains finite at zero twist.

### Why this matters for phonons

The two constituent monolayers have different phonon dispersions.

Their atomic masses and intralayer force constants differ.

Consequently, the moiré modulation can couple vibrational states belonging to different materials.

The resulting phonon modes may contain displacement components from both layers.

Whether substantial hybridization occurs depends on their frequencies, symmetry, and interlayer coupling strength.

The heterobilayer therefore introduces two distinct ingredients:

The moiré reciprocal lattice determines the momentum relationships between potentially coupled modes.

The differences between the constituent phonon spectra determine which modes are energetically close enough to interact strongly.

This provides the foundation for understanding interlayer phonon hybridization.

## 10. Why atomic reconstruction complicates the reciprocal-space picture

Yesterday, we examined how small-angle twisted bilayers can undergo atomic reconstruction.

The layers may relax into favorable stacking domains separated by domain walls.

This changes the spatial dependence of the interlayer force constants.

In the simplest rigid moiré model, the local registry varies smoothly across the moiré unit cell.

In a reconstructed structure, large domains can exhibit nearly uniform local registry, while much of the spatial variation is concentrated near domain boundaries.

The resulting modulation contains additional Fourier components beyond the lowest-order moiré reciprocal vectors.

This can influence the vibrational spectrum in several ways.

First, additional Fourier components can couple phonon states separated by larger combinations of moiré reciprocal vectors.

Second, the relaxed atomic positions modify the local dynamical matrix.

Third, the phonon eigenvectors may become concentrated in particular regions of the reconstructed structure.

Fourth, the symmetry and Raman matrix elements can change.

The resulting spectra can therefore differ substantially from predictions based on rigid geometrical folding.

This was demonstrated by Quan and colleagues in twisted bilayer MoS₂.

Their experiments revealed strong changes in phonon spectra at small twist angles.

The authors developed a continuum phonon model that incorporated spatially varying interlayer interactions and atomic reconstruction.

They showed that reconstruction and coupling between different phonon modes can substantially renormalize the vibrational spectrum.

The study demonstrated that Raman spectroscopy can provide information about nanoscale structural reconstruction even when the moiré unit cell is far smaller than the optical spatial resolution.

This is a particularly important result for experimental microscopy.

A diffraction-limited optical measurement can be sensitive to nanoscale structure through the vibrational eigenmodes and their spectral signatures.

The spatial resolution of the measurement and the structural length scale influencing the spectrum are different quantities.

## 11. How would you distinguish folding from genuine phonon renormalization?

Suppose a twisted bilayer exhibits several additional Raman peaks.

A useful analysis would begin with the phonon dispersions of the isolated constituent monolayers.

From the measured or estimated twist angle, calculate the moiré reciprocal vectors.

Next, identify the monolayer phonon frequencies at wavevectors corresponding to the relevant moiré reciprocal-vector combinations.

These provide approximate predictions for the frequencies of geometrically folded modes.

Now compare those predictions with the measured spectrum.

If the peak frequencies follow the expected monolayer dispersion as the twist angle changes, that supports a folding-based interpretation.

If the observed frequencies deviate substantially from the folded monolayer branches, additional dynamical effects may be important.

Possible explanations include interlayer phonon hybridization, structural relaxation, strain, and changes in local force constants.

### What would constitute stronger evidence for hybridization?

A convincing analysis should identify two parent vibrational branches with compatible symmetry.

It should then show how their eigenvectors mix as a parameter is varied.

An avoided crossing in the calculated or measured dispersion can support the interpretation.

Redistribution of spectral weight between the interacting branches provides complementary evidence.

However, intensity changes alone are insufficient because Raman matrix elements can vary independently of mode mixing.

For a reconstructed structure, calculated real-space displacement patterns are especially informative.

They can reveal whether a mode is extended across the moiré unit cell, concentrated within particular stacking domains, or associated with domain-wall motion.

These distinctions will become central in tomorrow's briefing.

## 12. Connection to phonon polaritons

There is a broader reason to understand moiré reciprocal vectors.

Phonon polaritons frequently possess in-plane momenta much larger than those of free-space photons at the same frequency.

This momentum mismatch makes direct far-field excitation inefficient in many geometries.

A periodic structure can provide additional in-plane momentum.

In principle, a moiré modulation of the dielectric response can scatter electromagnetic fields between wavevectors differing by moiré reciprocal vectors.

This provides a possible mechanism for coupling, scattering, or Bragg reflection of polaritonic modes.

The relevant condition depends on the polariton dispersion and the Fourier components of the dielectric modulation.

For example, consider a twisted polar heterostructure with a spatially varying infrared dielectric response.

If the dielectric modulation has a Fourier component at a wavevector comparable to the difference between two polariton wavevectors, it can couple those electromagnetic modes.

Repeated scattering in a sufficiently coherent periodic structure may produce polaritonic minibands or band gaps.

However, several conditions must be satisfied.

The dielectric modulation must have appreciable strength.

The polariton must propagate coherently over a sufficient distance.

The relevant electromagnetic modes must have compatible field profiles and polarization.

Material damping can also obscure the resulting band structure.

For twisted hBN, this distinction is especially important because the material's in-plane dielectric response is approximately isotropic at the macroscopic level.

Rotating two ideal hBN layers around their common out-of-plane axis does not rotate a distinct in-plane dielectric principal axis.

Twist-dependent polaritonic effects in such a system therefore require consideration of other mechanisms, including stacking-dependent microscopic response, reconstruction, and spatial dielectric modulation.

This differs from twisted stacks of strongly in-plane-anisotropic materials such as α-MoO₃, where relative rotation directly changes the orientation of the dielectric principal axes.

We will examine that distinction in Week 3.

## 13. Today's principal interpretive pitfall

### Misconception: Every phonon folded to the moiré-zone center becomes a strong Raman peak.

Zone folding changes the momentum classification of vibrational states.

Raman intensity is determined by the coupling between those states and the optical field.

A folded phonon can remain extremely weak if its Raman matrix element is small.

The actual intensity depends on the vibrational eigenvector, crystal symmetry, electronic resonance conditions, and interlayer coupling.

Furthermore, a geometrically folded mode may have a frequency close to that of its parent monolayer phonon.

A significant frequency shift requires a physical modification of the vibrational dynamics.

Thus, three questions must be considered separately:

**Where does the phonon appear in the reduced Brillouin zone?**

This is determined by reciprocal-space folding.

**How does its frequency change?**

This depends on the dynamical matrix and interactions between vibrational modes.

**How strongly does it appear in Raman spectroscopy?**

This depends on the optical matrix element and resonance conditions.

Keeping these questions separate is essential for evaluating experimental claims of moiré phonons.

## 14. Research exercise: Interpret a twist-dependent Raman spectrum

Imagine that you measure Raman spectra from three twisted MoS₂ bilayers.

Their twist angles are 0.5°, 1°, and 2°.

All three exhibit the conventional intralayer Raman modes.

The spectra also contain an additional low-frequency peak whose position changes systematically with twist angle.

### Question 1 — What is the first physical interpretation you should test?

**Answer:** Moiré-induced zone folding.

The twist angle determines the moiré reciprocal vectors.

A phonon originally located at a finite wavevector may become Raman-accessible when that wavevector is equivalent to the moiré-zone center.

The observed frequency should therefore be compared with the monolayer phonon dispersion evaluated at the corresponding wavevector.

For a folded acoustic phonon sufficiently close to the atomic Γ point, increasing twist angle generally selects a larger wavevector and therefore a higher phonon frequency.

The exact trend depends on the relevant branch and the moiré reciprocal-vector combination.

### Question 2 — Suppose the measured peak does not follow the predicted folded monolayer dispersion. What should you investigate?

**Answer:** The interlayer dynamical coupling and the relaxed atomic structure.

The moiré modulation can couple different phonon states.

At small twist angles, reconstruction may substantially modify local force constants and vibrational eigenvectors.

These effects can shift the frequencies away from the geometrical folding prediction.

A useful calculation would compare rigid and fully relaxed structures.

### Question 3 — The additional Raman peak becomes much stronger when the excitation wavelength is changed, but its frequency remains nearly constant. What does this suggest?

**Answer:** Resonance enhancement of the Raman matrix element.

The electronic intermediate states involved in Raman scattering depend on the excitation photon energy.

If the laser approaches an excitonic resonance, the scattering intensity can increase strongly.

This behavior was an important feature of the moiré-phonon measurements by Lin and colleagues.

A strong intensity enhancement therefore provides information about optical coupling but does not necessarily indicate a change in the phonon frequency.

### Question 4 — A MoS₂/WSe₂ heterobilayer exhibits a moiré pattern at zero twist. Is that possible?

**Answer:** Yes.

The two materials have different lattice constants.

Their lattice mismatch generates a spatial modulation of stacking registry even when their crystallographic orientations are aligned.

The resulting moiré reciprocal vectors depend on the difference between the constituent reciprocal lattices.

Twist provides an additional way to modify those vectors.

### Question 5 — Can you determine the local moiré period from a Raman spectrum measured with a diffraction-limited optical spot?

**Answer:** Potentially, provided a reliable relationship between the observed phonon features and the moiré period has been established.

Twist-dependent folded-phonon frequencies can act as spectral fingerprints of the moiré reciprocal vectors.

However, the measured spectrum averages over the illuminated region.

Spatial variations in twist angle, strain, or reconstruction may broaden peaks or produce multiple contributions.

The resulting estimate is therefore model-dependent and may represent an average over several local environments.

## 16. What you should retain

Today's central advance is the connection between twist geometry and phonon momentum.

Rotating two atomic lattices creates small difference vectors between their reciprocal lattices.

These difference vectors characterize the long-wavelength moiré modulation.

For commensurate structures, the enlarged real-space supercell produces a reduced Brillouin zone.

Phonons that occupied finite wavevectors in the original monolayer description can be folded toward the moiré-zone center.

Under suitable symmetry and optical resonance conditions, these vibrations can become observable in Raman spectroscopy.

The twist angle therefore provides a way to select different phonon wavevectors.

However, reciprocal-space folding and dynamical coupling have distinct consequences.

Folding changes the representation of the vibrational branches.

Interlayer coupling, strain, and reconstruction modify the dynamical matrix and can change the phonon frequencies and eigenvectors.

In heterobilayers, lattice mismatch introduces an additional contribution to the moiré reciprocal vectors.

This means that moiré periodicity and phonon folding can occur even at zero twist.

The same reciprocal-space concepts will later become important for understanding how periodic dielectric modulations can scatter or couple phonon-polariton modes.

**The most important principle is that the moiré reciprocal lattice determines which vibrational momenta can interact, while the microscopic force constants and optical matrix elements determine what happens when they do.**
