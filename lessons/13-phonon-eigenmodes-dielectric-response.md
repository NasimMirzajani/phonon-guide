# Weekend Physics Application 03
## From Vibrational Eigenmodes to Dielectric Response: Which Phonons Can Form Polaritons?

**Sunday, 25 October 2026 | Transition from Week 2 to Week 3 | Reading time: 9–10 minutes**

**Today's central question:** Suppose you have calculated the phonon frequencies and atomic displacement eigenvectors of a multilayer van der Waals material. How can you determine which vibrations contribute to its infrared dielectric response, which can couple to electromagnetic fields, and which can ultimately support phonon polaritons?

During Week 2, we examined how stacking registry, interlayer coupling, twist, and atomic reconstruction modify the vibrational eigenmodes of layered materials.

We now need to connect those atomic vibrations to electromagnetic fields.

The connection is particularly important for polar materials such as hexagonal boron nitride (hBN), where optical phonons produce strong infrared resonances and support highly confined electromagnetic modes.

Today, we will follow a specific physical sequence:

**Atomic displacement → oscillating polarization → dielectric response → electromagnetic eigenmodes.**

We will use bulk and few-layer hBN as our primary examples, then consider how the same reasoning extends to twisted heterostructures.

## 1. Begin with a phonon eigenvector

Consider a calculated optical phonon of hBN.

The calculation provides its frequency and the displacement of each atom within the unit cell.

For an in-plane optical vibration, boron and nitrogen atoms may move in opposite directions along the basal plane.

Because boron and nitrogen have different electronic environments, these displacements change the electric polarization of the material.

The important quantity is the **collective dipole moment generated by the phonon displacement**.

To understand why this matters, consider two possible vibrations.

In the first, the displacement of each atom produces a polarization contribution that reinforces the contributions from other atoms.

In the second, different atomic contributions cancel.

Both vibrations may have similar frequencies.

Both may involve substantial atomic motion.

But their coupling to an infrared electric field can be dramatically different.

This is the physical origin of infrared selection rules.

The central question is therefore:

**When the atoms move according to a particular phonon eigenvector, does the unit cell develop an oscillating electric dipole moment?**

---

## 2. Born effective charges: Connecting atomic displacement to polarization

The relevant quantity is the Born effective charge.

A Born effective charge describes how the macroscopic electric polarization changes when an atom is displaced.

It is generally a tensor.

This means that displacing an atom along one direction can produce a polarization change along another direction.

For a phonon mode, the individual atomic contributions must be combined according to the displacement eigenvector.

We define a mode-effective charge as

<div class="equation">

\[
\boxed{
\mathcal Z_{\nu,i}
=
\sum_{\kappa,j}
\frac{
Z^{*}_{\kappa,ij}\,
e_{\kappa j,\nu}
}{
\sqrt{M_\kappa}
}
}
\]

</div>

Here, \\(\nu\\) labels the phonon mode, \\(\kappa\\) labels atoms in the primitive cell, and \\(i,j\\) denote Cartesian directions. \\(Z^{*}_{\kappa,ij}\\) is the Born effective charge tensor of atom \\(\kappa\\), expressed in coulombs; \\(M_\kappa\\) is its mass; and \\(e_{\kappa j,\nu}\\) is its dimensionless displacement eigenvector component, normalized so that the sum of squared components over all atoms and directions equals one.

The resulting \\(\mathcal Z_{\nu,i}\\) describes the coupling of the mass-normalized vibrational coordinate to an electric field polarized along direction \\(i\\).

### Physical interpretation

Imagine applying an electric field along the x direction.

The field exerts forces on the atoms through their effective charges.

If those forces project strongly onto a particular phonon eigenvector, the field can efficiently excite that vibration.

If the projection vanishes, the mode is infrared-inactive for that field polarization.

The effective charge therefore determines the strength and direction of the phonon's infrared coupling.

This immediately explains why knowing the phonon frequency and eigenvector is insufficient to predict the infrared spectrum.

We also need the Born effective charges.

### What happens in a heterostructure?

Suppose we combine MoS₂ and WSe₂ into a bilayer.

The phonon eigenvectors may become mixtures of vibrations from both materials.

The mode-effective charge must then include the contributions from atoms in both layers.

Depending on their relative phases and displacement directions, those contributions can reinforce or cancel.

Hybridization can therefore redistribute infrared oscillator strength among the resulting phonon modes.

A vibration that becomes strongly mixed with an infrared-active mode may acquire infrared activity, provided the combined symmetry permits it.

This is one route through which interlayer coupling can modify the dielectric response.

---

## 3. A particularly useful example: Raman and infrared modes in bulk hBN

Bulk hBN provides an excellent demonstration of the distinction between atomic motion and infrared activity.

Its conventional centrosymmetric structure has four atoms in the primitive unit cell.

The optical phonons include modes with different combinations of in-plane and out-of-plane displacement.

Consider the high-frequency in-plane vibrations.

One mode has \\(E_{2g}\\) symmetry.

It is Raman-active.

Another has \\(E_{1u}\\) symmetry.

It is infrared-active.

Both involve substantial relative motion of boron and nitrogen atoms within the layers.

Their frequencies are also relatively close.

Yet their optical activities differ because the displacement patterns combine differently between layers.

### The Raman-active \\(E_{2g}\\) mode

For the high-frequency \\(E_{2g}\\) vibration, the atomic displacements produce a change in electronic polarizability.

The mode is therefore Raman-active.

Its contributions to the macroscopic dipole polarization cancel by symmetry.

Consequently, it has no electric-dipole infrared oscillator strength in the ideal centrosymmetric bulk crystal.

### The infrared-active \\(E_{1u}\\) mode

For the \\(E_{1u}\\) vibration, the displacement pattern produces a nonzero macroscopic in-plane polarization.

An in-plane infrared electric field can therefore excite it.

This vibration contributes strongly to the in-plane dielectric response.

Bulk hBN also has an infrared-active out-of-plane \\(A_{2u}\\) optical mode.

Its oscillating polarization is directed along the crystallographic c axis.

The distinction between these modes is central to understanding the dielectric anisotropy of hBN.

**The direction and symmetry of the phonon-induced polarization determine which component of the dielectric tensor contains the resonance.**

---

## 4. From mode-effective charge to dielectric response

We now have the ingredients needed to construct the infrared dielectric function.

An infrared-active phonon behaves approximately like a driven harmonic oscillator.

An applied electric field displaces the ions.

The restoring force produces a resonance near the transverse optical phonon frequency.

The oscillating ionic displacement generates polarization, which contributes to the dielectric response.

For a bulk insulating crystal, a simplified phonon contribution to the relative dielectric tensor can be written as

<div class="equation">

\[
\boxed{
\varepsilon_{ij}(\omega)
=
\varepsilon_{\infty,ij}
+
\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0 V}
\sum_{\nu}
\frac{
\mathcal Z_{\nu,i}\mathcal Z_{\nu,j}^{*}
}{
\omega_{\nu,\mathrm{TO}}^2-\omega^2-i\gamma_\nu\omega
}
}
\]

</div>

Here, \\(\varepsilon_{ij}(\omega)\\) is the frequency-dependent relative dielectric tensor; \\(\varepsilon_{\infty,ij}\\) is its electronic contribution; \\(\varepsilon_0\\) is the vacuum permittivity; and \\(V\\) is the primitive-cell volume.

The sum runs over infrared-active transverse optical phonons at the Brillouin-zone center. The quantity \\(\omega_{\nu,\mathrm{TO}}\\) is the transverse optical angular frequency, \\(\gamma_\nu\\) is its phenomenological damping rate, and \\(\omega\\) is the angular frequency of the applied field.

The mode-effective charges are defined using the normalization from Section 2.

This expression assumes a harmonic response and a simplified diagonal damping model.

For an isolated monolayer, the corresponding natural quantity is a two-dimensional sheet susceptibility. Assigning a bulk-like dielectric constant requires choosing an effective layer thickness.

### Three important consequences

**First: The phonon frequency determines where the dielectric resonance occurs.**

A lower-frequency infrared-active phonon contributes a resonance at lower photon energy.

**Second: The mode-effective charge determines the oscillator strength.**

A mode with a larger effective charge generally produces a stronger dielectric resonance.

**Third: The damping determines how sharply the response varies with frequency.**

A small damping rate produces a narrow resonance and potentially a large change in the real part of the dielectric function.

These three properties—frequency, oscillator strength, and damping—are essential for evaluating whether a material can support useful phonon polaritons.

## 5. Why the dielectric response becomes negative

Consider a single infrared-active optical phonon.

At frequencies well below its transverse optical resonance, the ionic polarization can follow the applied electric field approximately in phase.

As the driving frequency approaches the resonance, the ionic response becomes large and develops a substantial phase lag.

Above the resonance, the displacement response changes phase relative to the driving force.

This can produce a frequency interval in which the real part of the dielectric function becomes negative.

In a conventional bulk polar crystal, this interval is associated with the Reststrahlen band.

For a simple low-loss polar dielectric, the Reststrahlen region lies approximately between the transverse optical and longitudinal optical phonon frequencies.

The transverse optical frequency corresponds to a resonance of the dielectric response.

The longitudinal optical frequency is associated with a zero of the relevant dielectric response in the long-wavelength bulk limit.

The longitudinal mode generates a macroscopic electric field that contributes to its restoring force.

That additional electrostatic restoring force is responsible for LO–TO splitting.

### Why is a negative dielectric response important?

Maxwell's equations determine which electromagnetic waves can propagate through a material.

The dielectric function enters those equations directly.

A negative dielectric response can prevent ordinary propagating electromagnetic waves of certain polarizations while permitting surface-bound or strongly confined modes.

In anisotropic materials, the situation becomes especially interesting.

Different components of the dielectric tensor can have opposite signs.

That is the origin of hyperbolic electromagnetic dispersion.

---

## 6. Why hBN supports two different Reststrahlen bands

Bulk hBN is approximately isotropic within its basal plane but strongly anisotropic between the basal plane and the c axis.

Its dielectric tensor therefore has two independent principal components:

The in-plane component describes the response to electric fields parallel to the atomic layers.

The out-of-plane component describes the response to fields perpendicular to those layers.

The two components contain different infrared-active phonon resonances.

### Lower Reststrahlen band

The lower band occurs approximately between **760 and 825 cm⁻¹**, depending on the crystal and measurement conditions.

It originates primarily from the out-of-plane infrared-active optical phonon.

Within this band, the real part of the out-of-plane permittivity is negative, while the in-plane component remains positive.

This produces **type-I hyperbolic response**.

### Upper Reststrahlen band

The upper band occurs approximately between **1360 and 1610 cm⁻¹**.

It originates primarily from the in-plane infrared-active optical phonon.

Within this band, the real part of the in-plane permittivity is negative, while the out-of-plane component remains positive.

This produces **type-II hyperbolic response**.

These two spectral regions support different electromagnetic field distributions and propagation characteristics.

The hyperbolic behavior of hBN is reviewed extensively by Caldwell and colleagues. [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41578-019-0124-1)

### A useful physical interpretation

The two bands originate from different atomic displacement directions.

The lower-frequency out-of-plane phonon primarily controls the dielectric response along the c axis.

The higher-frequency in-plane phonon primarily controls the response within the basal plane.

The resulting anisotropy determines the directions in which electromagnetic fields can propagate.

This is a direct connection between the symmetry of an atomic displacement eigenvector and the geometry of an electromagnetic wave.

## 7. When does an infrared-active phonon form a phonon polariton?

We have now established that an infrared-active phonon contributes to the dielectric response.

The next question concerns the electromagnetic eigenmodes supported by that response.

A Phonon polariton is a coupled excitation involving an electromagnetic field and the polarization associated with an optical phonon.

Its dispersion is determined by solving Maxwell's equations using the material's frequency-dependent dielectric response.

The phonon contributes the resonant material polarization.

The electromagnetic field provides the propagating or confined wave component.

### Why are phonon polaritons especially interesting in layered materials?

Thin films and interfaces impose electromagnetic boundary conditions that can support strongly confined modes.

In a material such as hBN, the anisotropic dielectric response permits electromagnetic solutions with very large in-plane wavevectors.

These modes can have wavelengths far smaller than the corresponding free-space infrared wavelength.

Their field profiles can be strongly confined within or near the material.

The resulting polariton dispersion depends on the dielectric tensor, material thickness, surrounding media, and electromagnetic boundary conditions.

### Does every infrared-active phonon produce an observable polariton?

A nonzero mode-effective charge establishes coupling to an electric field.

The existence and observability of a particular propagating or confined polariton additionally depend on the electromagnetic geometry.

For example, a very thin polar layer may have a strong intrinsic infrared-active vibration but support a weakly confined electromagnetic mode under a particular set of conditions.

A polariton may also exist theoretically while being difficult to observe because of damping or momentum mismatch.

**The infrared oscillator strength determines the material coupling; the electromagnetic boundary-value problem determines the polaritonic modes.**

## 8. Why LO–TO splitting behaves differently in a monolayer

There is an important complication when moving from bulk crystals to atomically thin layers.

In a conventional three-dimensional polar crystal, the long-range electric field generated by a longitudinal optical phonon produces a finite LO–TO frequency splitting as the phonon wavevector approaches zero.

The situation changes in a strictly two-dimensional polar material.

For an isolated two-dimensional layer, the longitudinal and transverse optical phonon frequencies become degenerate in the limit of zero in-plane wavevector.

Nevertheless, the longitudinal mode acquires an additional contribution to its dispersion at finite wavevector.

The result is a finite slope difference near the zone center.

This was established by Sohier and colleagues using analytical models and density-functional perturbation theory.

They showed that the slope depends on the Born effective charges and dielectric screening.

The surrounding environment can substantially modify this behavior. [American Chemical Society Publications](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/abs/10.1021/acs.nanolett.7b01090)

### Physical intuition

Imagine a longitudinal optical vibration in which the induced polarization varies along the direction of propagation.

That polarization produces an electric field.

In a three-dimensional crystal, the field extends through a bulk medium and contributes a finite long-wavelength restoring force.

In a two-dimensional sheet, the electrostatic field extends into the surrounding space.

The resulting long-range Coulomb contribution depends differently on the in-plane wavevector.

As the wavevector approaches zero, the additional restoring force vanishes.

At finite wavevector, it becomes important.

This is why the LO and TO branches meet at the zone center but separate as the wavevector increases.

### What happens when the layer is placed on a substrate?

The surrounding dielectric environment screens the electric field generated by the longitudinal vibration.

Stronger screening reduces the electrostatic contribution to the restoring force.

Consequently, the longitudinal optical phonon dispersion changes.

Sohier and colleagues specifically examined the effect of dielectric screening in boron nitride–graphene heterostructures.

This provides a useful example of how forming a heterostructure can modify the phonon dispersion through electrostatic interactions, even when the short-range interatomic force constants change relatively little.

## 9. A concrete example: Graphene/hBN

Consider an hBN layer placed next to graphene.

The hBN provides polar optical phonons with substantial infrared oscillator strength.

Graphene provides an electronically polarizable layer whose response depends on carrier density.

The interaction between the two materials introduces several physical effects.

First, graphene can modify the dielectric screening experienced by the hBN phonons.

Second, the electromagnetic boundary conditions of the combined structure differ from those of isolated hBN.

Third, when graphene supports plasmons at frequencies overlapping the hBN phonon-polariton response, the electromagnetic modes can hybridize.

This produces mixed plasmon–phonon polaritons.

### Two different kinds of hybridization

We should distinguish two levels of coupling.

**Mechanical phonon hybridization** occurs when the interatomic force constants mix vibrational displacement eigenvectors.

For example, an interlayer interaction can mix phonon modes from two adjacent crystals.

**Electromagnetic polariton hybridization** occurs when electromagnetic modes interact through the frequency-dependent dielectric response.

In graphene/hBN, the plasmonic response of graphene and the phonon-mediated dielectric response of hBN can couple electromagnetically.

This can produce hybrid polaritonic modes even when the corresponding atomic vibrations remain predominantly associated with hBN.

The distinction is important when interpreting experimental dispersions.

An avoided crossing in a polariton dispersion may indicate electromagnetic mode hybridization.

It does not, by itself, establish strong mixing between the underlying atomic phonon eigenvectors.

## 10. What can Raman, infrared, and near-field measurements tell us?

Suppose we want to investigate the relationship between phonons and polaritons in an hBN heterostructure.

Different experimental techniques constrain different aspects of the problem.

| Measurement | Primary observable | Main physical information |
|---|---|---|
| Raman spectroscopy | Phonon frequencies, linewidths, Raman tensors | Vibrational symmetry, strain, and changes in polarizability |
| Far-field infrared spectroscopy | Absorption, reflection, transmission | Infrared oscillator strength and dielectric resonances |
| Infrared ellipsometry | Polarization-dependent optical response | Components of the dielectric tensor |
| s-SNOM | Complex near-field scattering response | Local dielectric contrast and confined electromagnetic modes |
| Nano-FTIR | Frequency-resolved near-field response | Nanoscale infrared resonances |
| EELS | Momentum- and energy-dependent loss probability | Vibrational and electromagnetic excitations, depending on geometry and momentum |

The distinction between far-field and near-field momentum access is particularly important.

### Far-field infrared spectroscopy

A conventional infrared photon carries relatively little in-plane momentum.

The measurement therefore probes the optical response near zero in-plane wavevector.

The observed resonances provide information about infrared-active phonons and the dielectric function.

### Near-field microscopy

A sharp metallic tip produces electromagnetic fields containing a broad distribution of in-plane wavevectors.

These fields can couple to polaritons with momenta much larger than those of free-space photons.

The tip can launch a polariton that propagates across the sample.

If the polariton reflects from an edge or defect, the incident and reflected fields can interfere.

This produces spatial fringes in the near-field signal.

By measuring their spacing and frequency dependence, we can extract information about the polariton wavevector and dispersion.

### Why is this different from imaging a phonon?

The near-field fringes primarily reveal the spatial structure of the electromagnetic mode.

They provide information about the polariton wavelength, propagation, and damping.

The atomic phonon contributes through the dielectric response.

To determine the underlying phonon displacement eigenvector, we generally need complementary lattice-dynamical calculations or vibrational measurements.

## 11. Returning to twisted hBN: What could twist change?

We can now revisit the central theme of the curriculum.

Suppose two hBN layers are twisted relative to one another.

Their local stacking registry varies across the interface.

Depending on twist angle and relaxation, the structure may develop a spatially varying distribution of interlayer force constants.

This can modify the phonon eigenvectors and frequencies.

It may also change their infrared oscillator strengths.

### Mechanism 1: Mechanical coupling

The interlayer interaction modifies the dynamical matrix.

Phonon branches can shift or hybridize.

The resulting eigenvectors may have different relative displacements between the layers.

### Mechanism 2: Symmetry

The stacking arrangement determines the symmetry of the combined structure.

Changes in symmetry can alter which vibrational modes are infrared-active.

Modes that are forbidden in a higher-symmetry reference structure may become allowed when the symmetry is reduced.

### Mechanism 3: Spatially varying dielectric response

If the local stacking environment modifies the infrared response, the dielectric properties may vary across the moiré structure.

Such modulation can scatter or couple electromagnetic modes whose wavevectors differ by moiré reciprocal vectors.

### Mechanism 4: Electromagnetic coupling between layers

The electromagnetic modes of the combined structure depend on the dielectric properties of both layers and their separation.

Their coupling can produce changes in polariton dispersion even when the individual phonon frequencies remain nearly unchanged.

### An important distinction for hBN

Bulk hBN is approximately isotropic within its basal plane.

Rotating one ideal hBN layer around the common out-of-plane axis therefore does not rotate a distinct in-plane principal dielectric axis.

Twist-dependent polaritonic effects in hBN must be understood through mechanisms such as stacking-dependent microscopic response, structural reconstruction, or electromagnetic coupling.

This differs from twisted α-MoO₃ stacks, where the in-plane dielectric tensor is strongly anisotropic.

In α-MoO₃, rotating one layer directly changes the relative orientations of the dielectric principal axes.

That distinction will become important when we study twist-tunable hyperbolic polaritons.

## 12. Today's principal interpretive pitfall

### Misconception: A strong infrared-active phonon necessarily produces a strongly confined phonon polariton.

A large infrared oscillator strength indicates strong coupling between an atomic vibration and an electric field.

The polariton dispersion also depends on the full dielectric response and the electromagnetic geometry.

In particular, strong confinement requires an electromagnetic solution with a large in-plane wavevector.

The existence of that solution depends on the dielectric tensor, frequency, thickness, and surrounding media.

Damping determines how far the resulting mode can propagate.

Thus, a material may exhibit a strong infrared absorption resonance without supporting a long-lived, highly confined polariton in a particular geometry.

Conversely, a relatively modest modification of the dielectric function can produce a substantial change in polariton propagation if the electromagnetic mode is particularly sensitive to that parameter.

**Infrared oscillator strength, electromagnetic confinement, and polariton propagation length are separate physical observables.**

## 13. Research exercise: Four calculated phonons

Suppose a first-principles calculation of an hBN-based heterostructure produces four optical phonon modes.

### Mode A

The atoms undergo a substantial in-plane vibration.

The mode-effective charge vanishes because the polarization contributions cancel.

**Question:** Should this mode produce a strong electric-dipole infrared absorption peak?

**Answer:** No. Its vanishing mode-effective charge means that it has no electric-dipole infrared oscillator strength in the ideal calculated structure.

It may still be Raman-active if its displacement changes the electronic polarizability.

### Mode B

The atoms undergo an in-plane vibration with a large mode-effective charge.

**Question:** Which dielectric tensor component should exhibit a resonance?

**Answer:** The in-plane component, provided the induced polarization lies within the basal plane.

For hBN, the strong in-plane infrared-active phonon is responsible for the upper Reststrahlen band.

### Mode C

The vibration is predominantly out of plane and produces a substantial polarization along the c axis.

**Question:** Which Reststrahlen band is relevant?

**Answer:** The lower Reststrahlen band of hBN, associated with its out-of-plane infrared-active optical phonon.

The negative out-of-plane dielectric response produces type-I hyperbolic behavior in the corresponding frequency interval.

### Mode D

Two initially distinct phonons hybridize when the layers are brought together.

Their frequencies shift slightly, but one becomes much more infrared-active.

**Question:** What has changed?

**Answer:** The hybridized displacement eigenvectors have changed the collective mode-effective charges.

The new combinations of atomic motion produce different degrees of constructive or destructive interference among the polarization contributions.

The resulting redistribution of oscillator strength can modify the infrared spectrum even when the frequency shifts are relatively small.

**Sohier, Gibertini, Calandra, Mauri, and Marzari, “Breakdown of Optical Phonons’ Splitting in Two-Dimensional Materials.”**

*Nano Letters* **17**, 3758–3763 (2017).

[Open-access preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/1612.07191)

**Why this paper matters**

This paper establishes how the long-range electrostatic contribution to optical phonon dispersion changes between three-dimensional and two-dimensional polar materials.

The authors demonstrate that the LO–TO frequency splitting vanishes at the zone center in an ideal two-dimensional material.

The longitudinal optical branch nevertheless acquires a finite slope determined by Born effective charges and dielectric screening.

The work also examines multilayers and the screening effects of surrounding materials, including boron nitride–graphene heterostructures.

For today's application, focus on how the authors connect the phonon-induced polarization to the macroscopic electric field.

Pay particular attention to the distinction between short-range interatomic force constants and long-range Coulomb interactions.

**Research question:** How can placing a polar monolayer next to graphene change its longitudinal optical phonon dispersion without substantially changing its local bonding geometry?

**Caldwell et al., “Photonics with hexagonal boron nitride.”**

[Read the paper — DOI: 10.1038/s41578-019-0124-1](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41578-019-0124-1)

This review connects the vibrational and dielectric properties of hBN to its nanophotonic behavior.

## 15. What you should retain

The connection between atomic vibrations and phonon polaritons begins with the phonon-induced electric polarization.

A phonon eigenvector describes the relative atomic displacements.

The Born effective charges determine how those displacements generate macroscopic polarization.

Combining the eigenvectors with the Born effective charges gives the mode-effective charge.

The mode-effective charge determines the infrared oscillator strength and the direction of the phonon's coupling to an electric field.

Infrared-active phonons contribute resonances to the dielectric tensor.

Near these resonances, the real part of a dielectric tensor component can become negative.

In anisotropic materials such as hBN, different infrared-active phonons contribute to different tensor components.

This produces distinct Reststrahlen bands and hyperbolic electromagnetic responses.

The electromagnetic modes supported by a material are then determined by Maxwell's equations and the relevant boundary conditions.

In a heterostructure, interlayer mechanical coupling can modify phonon eigenvectors and oscillator strengths.

The electromagnetic coupling between layers can independently modify polariton dispersion and confinement.

**The central principle is that phonon eigenvectors determine the microscopic polarization response, while the dielectric tensor and electromagnetic geometry determine the resulting polariton modes.**

This provides the conceptual bridge from the lattice dynamics of twisted heterostructures to their polaritonic properties.
