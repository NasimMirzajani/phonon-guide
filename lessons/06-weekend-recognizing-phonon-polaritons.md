# Morning Physics Briefing — Sunday Research Application
## 
From Atomic Vibrations to Electromagnetic Waves: How to Recognize a Phonon Polariton Experimentally

Sunday, 18 October 2026 | Week 1 — Consolidation and Research Application | Reading time: 9–10 minutes

**Today's central question:**

*You measure an infrared resonance in a layered crystal. How do you determine whether you are observing an ordinary optical phonon, a dielectric resonance, or a propagating phonon polariton?*

Yesterday, we examined how stacking registry, interlayer force constants, and symmetry determine the vibrational spectrum of a multilayer structure.

Today, we will connect those ideas to electromagnetic response.

This is an important conceptual transition. In the coming weeks, we will investigate how optical phonons hybridize with electromagnetic fields, how anisotropic dielectric tensors produce hyperbolic propagation, and how twisting layered crystals modifies these excitations.

Before studying those phenomena, we need to distinguish three related but different physical objects:

- An optical phonon: a collective lattice vibration.

- A dielectric resonance: a frequency-dependent electromagnetic response generated by material excitations.

- A phonon polariton: a coupled excitation involving electromagnetic fields and polar lattice vibrations.

These are closely connected, but they are not interchangeable.

## 1. Begin with a polar optical phonon

Consider a crystal containing positively and negatively charged atomic sublattices.

An optical phonon can involve relative displacement of these sublattices.

In a polar material, such motion can produce an oscillating electric polarization.

For example, in hexagonal boron nitride (hBN), boron and nitrogen atoms have different effective charges.

Certain optical phonons therefore generate oscillating electric dipoles.

These dipoles interact with electromagnetic fields.

This is fundamentally different from an acoustic phonon near the Brillouin-zone center, where the atoms move approximately together and the macroscopic polarization generally vanishes.

However, not every optical phonon is infrared-active.

Whether a vibration couples to a macroscopic electric field depends on its symmetry and mode-effective charge.

This connects directly to Friday's discussion of Raman and infrared selection rules.

**An optical phonon becomes important for infrared electrodynamics when its collective atomic displacement produces a nonzero oscillating macroscopic polarization.**

The relevant quantity is not simply how far the atoms move, but how their motion redistributes electric charge.

## 2. The physical origin of dielectric dispersion

Imagine applying an oscillating electric field to a polar crystal.

At frequencies far below an optical phonon resonance, the ionic sublattices can respond relatively easily to the field.

As the driving frequency approaches the phonon frequency, the ionic displacement becomes resonantly enhanced.

Above the resonance, the ionic motion can no longer follow the field in phase.

This produces a strongly frequency-dependent dielectric response.

The dielectric function is therefore not merely a background material parameter.

It contains information about the collective excitations of the crystal.

A useful description is the Lorentz-oscillator model.

For a principal crystallographic direction, the dielectric response can be approximated by

<div class="equation">
\[
\boxed{ \varepsilon_j(\omega) = \varepsilon_{\infty,j} \frac{ \omega_{\mathrm{LO},j}^{2}-\omega^{2}-i\gamma_j\omega }{ \omega_{\mathrm{TO},j}^{2}-\omega^{2}-i\gamma_j\omega } }
\]
</div>

Here:

<!-- -->

- ε_j is the complex dielectric function along principal axis j.

- ω is the angular frequency of the electromagnetic field.

- ε∞,j is the high-frequency dielectric constant associated with electronic polarization.

- ω_TO,j is the transverse-optical phonon frequency.

- ω_LO,j is the longitudinal-optical phonon frequency.

- γ_j is the phenomenological damping rate.

This is a simplified single-oscillator representation. It assumes one dominant infrared-active phonon for the chosen polarization and uses a common damping parameter in the numerator and denominator.

More complicated materials may require several oscillators.

The physical message is more important than the detailed expression.

**The dielectric response changes rapidly near an infrared-active optical phonon because the ionic polarization becomes resonant.**

Under sufficiently weak damping, the real part of the dielectric function can become negative between the transverse- and longitudinal-optical phonon frequencies.

This interval is called a Reststrahlen band.

We will examine its electrodynamic origin more carefully in Monday's briefing.

## 3. Why a negative dielectric function matters

Consider an electromagnetic wave approaching a material.

Its propagation depends on the material's dielectric response.

In an ordinary transparent dielectric, the real part of the permittivity is positive.

In a polar crystal near an optical phonon resonance, the ionic polarization can oppose the applied electric field strongly enough that the real part of the permittivity becomes negative.

This changes the electromagnetic boundary-value problem.

At an interface between a positive-permittivity medium and a material with an appropriate negative dielectric response, electromagnetic fields can become confined near the interface.

Under suitable conditions, the resulting excitation is a surface phonon polariton.

The electromagnetic field is accompanied by oscillating ionic polarization in the crystal.

The two components form a coupled excitation.

It is useful to think of this as an electromagnetic wave that continually drives the lattice, while the lattice polarization continually modifies the electromagnetic field.

Neither component can be treated independently when the coupling is strong.

This is the essential physical picture of a Phonon polariton.

Importantly, a negative dielectric function does not automatically imply that every geometry supports a propagating surface polariton.

The existence and dispersion of the mode depend on the interface, electromagnetic polarization, material losses, and boundary conditions.

## 4. A concrete material: Hexagonal boron nitride

Hexagonal boron nitride is an especially useful starting point because it combines several properties relevant to our curriculum.

It is a layered van der Waals crystal.

Its in-plane bonding is strong, while adjacent layers interact much more weakly.

It contains polar B–N bonds.

And its dielectric response is strongly anisotropic.

The final property is particularly important.

An electric field parallel to the layers produces a different ionic response from an electric field perpendicular to them.

Consequently, the dielectric response cannot generally be described by a single scalar function.

Instead, hBN is approximately uniaxial, with two independent principal dielectric components.

We denote them ε∥ for the in-plane response and ε⊥ for the out-of-plane response.

Each component is associated with different infrared-active optical phonons.

As a result, the frequencies at which the in-plane dielectric response becomes negative differ from those at which the out-of-plane response becomes negative.

This produces two distinct Reststrahlen bands.

In the lower-frequency band, the out-of-plane dielectric component is negative while the in-plane component remains positive.

In the higher-frequency band, the in-plane component is negative while the out-of-plane component remains positive.

The precise frequency intervals depend on the material and its phonon parameters.

**The crucial feature is that the principal dielectric components can have opposite signs at the same frequency.**

This is the defining electromagnetic property of a hyperbolic medium.

## 5. Why opposite dielectric signs produce unusual propagation

In an isotropic dielectric, the allowed wavevectors of electromagnetic waves at a fixed frequency form a spherical surface in reciprocal space.

Anisotropy changes this geometry.

For an ideal uniaxial medium, the extraordinary-wave dispersion relation is

<div class="equation">
\[
\boxed{ \frac{q_{\parallel}^{2}}{\varepsilon_{\perp}} + \frac{q_{z}^{2}}{\varepsilon_{\parallel}} = \frac{\omega^{2}}{c^{2}} }
\]
</div>

Here:

<!-- -->

- q∥ is the magnitude of the in-plane wavevector.

- q_z is the out-of-plane wavevector component.

- ε∥ and ε⊥ are the corresponding principal dielectric functions.

- ω is the electromagnetic angular frequency.

- c is the speed of light in vacuum.

This expression describes extraordinary electromagnetic waves in a homogeneous, local, uniaxial medium.

If both dielectric components are positive, the constant-frequency surface is ellipsoidal.

If their real parts have opposite signs, the corresponding lossless dispersion surface becomes hyperbolic.

This is the origin of Hyperbolic dispersion.

The physical consequence is remarkable.

A hyperbolic material can support propagating electromagnetic excitations with very large wavevectors.

These correspond to wavelengths much shorter than the wavelength of light in free space at the same frequency.

In a finite hBN slab, electromagnetic boundary conditions select guided polariton modes from this bulk response.

Their dispersion depends on slab thickness, surrounding dielectric materials, and mode order.

This is why the thickness of an hBN crystal can strongly affect its phonon-polariton wavelengths.

## 6. Research application: An hBN flake on a substrate

Imagine that your laboratory has fabricated an hBN flake on a dielectric substrate.

You want to determine whether the material supports propagating phonon polaritons.

You have access to three experimental techniques:

**Far-field infrared spectroscopy, Raman spectroscopy, and scattering-type scanning near-field optical microscopy (s-SNOM).**

Each measurement probes a different aspect of the system.

**Measurement A: Far-field infrared spectroscopy**

You illuminate the sample with infrared radiation and measure its reflection or transmission.

Near an infrared-active optical phonon, the spectrum can exhibit a strong resonance or a region of enhanced reflectivity.

This establishes that the material possesses a significant infrared dielectric response.

With suitable optical modeling, the measurement can help determine phonon frequencies, damping rates, and dielectric parameters.

However, an infrared resonance alone does not demonstrate that a propagating phonon polariton has been excited.

It primarily characterizes the frequency-dependent optical response accessible to the incident field.

The in-plane momentum of ordinary far-field light is relatively small.

Many highly confined polariton modes possess much larger in-plane wavevectors.

Consequently, direct excitation of these modes by a plane wave can be strongly momentum-mismatched.

**Measurement B: Raman spectroscopy**

Now measure the vibrational spectrum using Raman scattering.

You may observe optical phonons that modulate the electronic polarizability.

These provide information about lattice symmetry, strain, stacking, and other structural properties.

But the strongest Raman-active phonon is not necessarily the phonon that dominates the infrared dielectric response.

As established in Briefing 04, Raman and infrared selection rules are different.

A mode can be prominent in Raman spectroscopy while contributing little to the infrared oscillator strength.

This is an important reason not to identify the polaritonic response from Raman spectra alone.

**Measurement C: Infrared near-field microscopy**

Finally, examine the hBN flake using s-SNOM.

In this technique, a sharp metallic tip concentrates the incident infrared field into a nanoscale region.

The tip provides a broad distribution of in-plane momenta.

This allows coupling to polaritonic modes that ordinary far-field illumination cannot efficiently excite.

The tip can also scatter part of the local near field into detectable radiation.

Near an edge or another scattering feature, the launched polariton may be reflected.

Interference between the outgoing and reflected polariton fields can produce spatial fringes in the measured near-field signal.

The fringe spacing contains information about the polariton wavelength.

However, the relationship between fringe spacing and polariton wavelength depends on the launching and detection geometry.

For a simple standing-wave pattern produced by tip-launched polaritons reflecting from an edge, adjacent intensity maxima are commonly separated by approximately half the polariton wavelength.

Other interference configurations can produce different relationships.

**Observing spatially propagating fringes with frequency-dependent wavelengths provides much stronger evidence for a polariton than observing an infrared absorption peak alone.**

## 7. What did the foundational experiments demonstrate?

In 2014, Dai and colleagues used infrared nanoimaging to investigate phonon polaritons in thin hBN crystals.

Their experiments demonstrated that polaritonic waves could be launched and imaged directly.

An important result was that the polariton wavelength depended strongly on the thickness of the hBN.

The thickness dependence persisted down to crystals only a few atomic layers thick.

This result is significant because the optical phonon frequencies of the constituent crystal do not need to change dramatically for the electromagnetic mode dispersion to change substantially.

The geometry of the material modifies the electromagnetic boundary conditions and therefore the allowed polariton wavevectors.

This illustrates a central distinction between phonons and phonon polaritons.

**The phonon frequency is primarily determined by lattice dynamics, whereas the polariton dispersion also depends on electromagnetic confinement and boundary conditions.**

A related experiment by Caldwell and colleagues demonstrated deeply subwavelength, volume-confined hyperbolic phonon-polariton resonances in hBN nanostructures.

Their work established that the natural dielectric anisotropy of hBN could support highly confined electromagnetic excitations without constructing an artificial metal–dielectric metamaterial. <u>[MIT Open Scholarship](https://dspace.mit.edu/entities/publication/6ad9ae2c-27e6-4259-9c52-7051a3318cf2?utm_source=chatgpt.com)</u>

## 8. Now construct a graphene/hBN heterostructure

Suppose we place a graphene layer on top of the hBN flake.

The system now contains two materials with different electromagnetic excitations.

Graphene supports electronic plasmons associated with collective oscillations of its charge carriers.

hBN supports phonon polaritons associated with polar lattice vibrations.

When their electromagnetic fields overlap, the excitations can interact.

The resulting modes may exhibit mixed plasmonic and phononic character.

This is particularly interesting because graphene's carrier density can be controlled electrically.

Changing the carrier density changes the graphene plasmon dispersion.

The hBN phonon frequencies, by contrast, are comparatively insensitive to electrostatic gating under ordinary conditions.

Consequently, gating can modify the hybrid electromagnetic modes without requiring a corresponding large change in the underlying hBN lattice frequencies.

This distinction is extremely useful when interpreting heterostructure experiments.

Imagine observing a strong shift in a near-field resonance after changing the graphene carrier density.

It would be incorrect to conclude immediately that the hBN optical phonon frequency has shifted by the same amount.

The measured change might instead result from altered electromagnetic mode hybridization.

To establish hybridization convincingly, one should examine how the modes evolve as a tuning parameter brings the uncoupled excitations toward resonance.

Avoided crossings, redistribution of spectral weight, and agreement with a coupled electrodynamic model can provide evidence.

However, an apparent avoided crossing must be interpreted carefully when the modes are strongly damped or when multiple optical resonances overlap.

A realistic model should account for the complex dielectric functions and the conductivity of graphene.

## 9. What happens if the layers are twisted?

Now imagine rotating one component of a multilayer heterostructure relative to another.

We already know from Saturday's application that twisting can change local atomic registry, interlayer forces, and phonon selection rules.

But there is another possibility.

Twisting can change the relative orientation of anisotropic electromagnetic responses.

This is particularly important when the constituent layers are anisotropic within their planes.

Consider two α-MoO₃ crystals stacked with a relative rotation.

Unlike hBN, α-MoO₃ possesses strongly different dielectric responses along its principal in-plane crystallographic directions.

Rotating one layer therefore rotates its in-plane dielectric principal axes relative to those of the other.

The electromagnetic boundary conditions now couple two differently oriented anisotropic media.

The resulting polaritonic dispersion can change with twist angle.

This provides a mechanism for twist-dependent polariton propagation that is distinct from conventional moiré-induced changes in atomic force constants.

The distinction is essential:

**Twist-dependent lattice dynamics and twist-dependent electromagnetic mode coupling are different physical mechanisms.**

They may coexist in a real heterostructure, but one should not assume that observing twist-dependent polariton propagation proves the existence of moiré-modified phonon eigenfrequencies.

In an ideal hBN/hBN stack, rotating one layer around the common out-of-plane axis does not rotate a distinct in-plane dielectric principal axis, because the in-plane dielectric response is approximately isotropic.

Twisting hBN layers can still affect interlayer interactions, local symmetry, reconstruction, and potentially the polaritonic response.

But the mechanism is not identical to rotating two intrinsically in-plane anisotropic α-MoO₃ layers.

We will return to this distinction in Week 3.

## 10. Today's principal interpretive pitfall

Suppose a paper reports that a new infrared resonance appears after stacking two van der Waals crystals.

The authors claim that this demonstrates a new interface phonon.

Is that conclusion justified?

Not necessarily.

A new optical resonance can arise from several different mechanisms.

It may correspond to a genuine vibrational eigenmode localized near the interface.

It may result from electromagnetic hybridization between existing polaritonic modes.

It may arise because the new dielectric environment changes the optical boundary conditions.

Or it may reflect a previously weak resonance becoming observable through altered field confinement or selection rules.

These possibilities require different theoretical descriptions.

A genuine interface-localized phonon is primarily a lattice-dynamical excitation whose displacement pattern is concentrated near the interface.

A hybrid electromagnetic mode may exhibit strong interface confinement even when the underlying lattice vibrations are largely bulk-like.

The distinction cannot always be established from optical spectra alone.

To identify a genuinely new interface vibrational mode, it is useful to examine calculated phonon eigenvectors and their spatial localization.

To establish polaritonic hybridization, one should analyze electromagnetic field profiles, dispersion, and mode coupling.

**Spatial localization of an electromagnetic field does not, by itself, demonstrate localization of the underlying atomic vibration.**

This is a particularly important caution when evaluating claims about interface modes in twisted multilayer structures.

## 11. Research exercise: Interpret three observations

Consider a hypothetical hBN-based heterostructure.

You perform infrared spectroscopy, Raman spectroscopy, and s-SNOM.

**Observation 1**

A strong infrared resonance occurs near an optical phonon frequency, but no propagating fringes are detected in the near-field images.

**Question:** Can you conclude that the material does not support phonon polaritons?

**Answer:** No.

The absence of observable fringes does not establish the absence of a supported polaritonic mode.

The mode may be poorly excited by the tip, strongly damped, or inaccessible within the available frequency range.

Its propagation length may also be too short to produce resolvable interference.

A complete assessment requires considering the electromagnetic dispersion, excitation efficiency, damping, and experimental geometry.

**Observation 2**

The hBN thickness is reduced, and the near-field polariton wavelength changes substantially. The principal Raman-active phonon frequencies remain nearly unchanged.

**Question:** Is this contradictory?

**Answer:** No.

The polariton wavelength depends on electromagnetic confinement and the boundary conditions of the finite-thickness crystal.

The Raman-active phonon frequencies primarily reflect the lattice restoring forces and vibrational eigenvectors.

A substantial change in polariton dispersion can therefore occur without an equally large change in the measured phonon frequencies.

This is closely related to the thickness-dependent polariton behavior demonstrated by Dai and colleagues.

**Observation 3**

A graphene/hBN heterostructure exhibits a gate-dependent near-field resonance.

**Question:** Does this prove that the hBN phonon frequency is electrically tunable?

**Answer:** No.

The gate may primarily modify the graphene electronic conductivity and plasmon dispersion.

This can shift the frequencies and wavevectors of hybrid plasmon–phonon-polariton modes without significantly modifying the bare hBN phonon frequencies.

To distinguish these effects, one should compare the measured response with a model containing the separate graphene and hBN electromagnetic properties.

A particularly useful test is to examine whether the gate-dependent spectra exhibit the expected behavior of coupled modes.

## 13. What you should retain

Today's application establishes an important hierarchy of physical descriptions.

At the most microscopic level, **the lattice dynamical matrix determines the optical phonon eigenfrequencies and displacement patterns**.

The mode-effective charges determine how strongly those vibrations contribute to the macroscopic polarization.

The resulting dielectric response describes how the material interacts with electromagnetic fields.

Finally, electromagnetic boundary conditions determine which coupled phonon–photon modes can propagate in a particular geometry.

These levels are connected, but they should not be confused.

An optical phonon resonance is not automatically evidence of a propagating phonon polariton.

A change in polariton wavelength does not necessarily imply a corresponding change in the bare phonon frequency.

And a localized electromagnetic field does not necessarily indicate a new interface-localized lattice vibration.

These distinctions become particularly important in twisted and multilayer heterostructures, where changes in stacking, dielectric environment, symmetry, and electromagnetic coupling can occur simultaneously.

**The central lesson is that interpreting phonon-polariton experiments requires both lattice dynamics and electrodynamics. Neither description is sufficient on its own.**
